A new study suggests that taxi and ambulance drivers have a lower mortality rate from Alzheimer's disease compared to the general population, with about a 40% reduction in risk. However, the effect is modest and may be influenced by confounding factors such as life expectancy and selection bias. This finding adds to the growing evidence that complex spatial reasoning and navigation may protect against cognitive decline, but it also highlights the importance of rigorous epidemiological methods. The study could influence future research on cognitive health and occupational factors, though caution is needed before drawing causal conclusions. The study found that taxi and ambulance drivers had a 40% lower risk of dying from Alzheimer's compared to the average, but the absolute risk reduction was from 1 in 60 to 1 in 100. The effect is partly attributed to the unique spatial memory training, such as 'The Knowledge' required for London cabbies, but selection bias and shorter life expectancy in these occupations may confound the results.

Epidemiological studies often face challenges like selection bias, where the study population may not be representative, and confounding, where other factors influence the outcome. The 'healthy worker effect' is a type of selection bias where workers are generally healthier than the general population, which could affect mortality comparisons.
